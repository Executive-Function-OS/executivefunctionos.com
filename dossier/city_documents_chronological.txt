On June 24, 2024, the City of Oregon City terminated water service to my
residence with only same-day notice, leaving me without access to water for
over 395 days. The termination occurred despite minimal arrearages and my
attempts to negotiate payment. The City subsequently escalated reinstatement
fees, removed the water meter, and, on July 10, 2024, placed a concrete
block over the supply pipes to obstruct restoration. On October 1, 2024, the
City designated my continuously occupied home as "uninhabitable" with a "Do
Not Enter" sign, forcing me to source water primitively and subjecting me to
public humiliation. The stress from these actions worsened a diagnosed
hyperthyroidism condition and led to dehydration when I contracted COVID-19.
I have also suffered from major depression and social isolation. The City's
punitive actions appear retaliatory, coinciding with the property's involvement
in quiet title litigation.

The statutory notice period under ORS 30.275 is tolled due to:

* The continuing violation, as the City's refusal to restore water service
  constitutes an ongoing tort.

* The discovery rule, as the full scope of retaliatory conduct was revealed
  through recent records.

* The impact of Claimant's disability and compounded distress, which were
  exacerbated by the City's actions and impeded earlier filing.

MOTION TO STRIKE

Pursuant to UTCR 5.030, Defendant City of Oregon City (hereinafter "Defendant" or
"the City") respectfully moves the Court to strike proposed intervenor Donald Buckhout's Reply
in support of his Motion to Intervene as untimely. This motion relies on the pleadings on file
with the Court, the Declaration of Patrick Foiles, and the points and authorities set forth below.

POINTS AND AUTHORITIES

UTCR 5.030 sets out the briefing timeline for motions other than those for summary
judgment. Once filed or served, whichever is later, the nonmoving party has 14 days to file an
opposition response brief, and the moving party (here, Mr. Buckhout) has seven days to file and
serve a reply brief. UTCR 5.030(1)-(2). An additional three days may be added to these timelines
if service occurs in the manner prescribed by ORCP 10 B.

Mr. Buckhout filed his Motion for Leave to Intervene, Ex Parte Temporary Restraining
Order, Order to Show Cause, and Request for Judicial Notice on August 5, 2025. However, the
City was not served with or made aware of Mr. Buckhout's Motions until August 20, 2025.
Foiles Decl. at 2-3. The City filed and served its Response in Opposition on August 28, 2025.
Accordingly, Mr. Buckhout had seven days to file and serve a Reply. Because the City served
Mr. Buckhout via U.S. Mail and electronic mail, an additional three days was added to the time
for Reply. ORCP 10 B. The last day of the period so computed shall be included, unless it is a
Saturday or a legal holiday, including Sunday, in which event the period runs until the end of the
next day that is not a Saturday or a legal holiday. ORCP 10. Here, the last day of the 10-day
period in which Mr. Buckhout had to file a reply fell on Sunday, September 7. Accounting for
the Sunday holiday, Mr. Buckhout had until Monday, September 8, 2025 to file and serve their
Reply brief. Mr. Buckhout failed to do. Instead, Mr. Buckhout filed and served a Reply on
October 2, 2025, more than three weeks after it was due. There is no indication Mr. Buckhout
requested the Court modify the briefing schedule or time periods. Therefore, his Reply is
untimely and should be stricken.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, defendant respectfully moves the Court to strike
Mr. Buckhout's Reply as untimely.

INTRODUCTION

Proposed Intervenor Donald Buckhout ("Movant") respectfully submits this Reply to the
Response filed by the City of Oregon City ("City"). The City's response opposes the Movant's
motion for intervention and emergency relief through circular arguments, contending that the
Movant lacks standing as a non-party and that his claims are meritless. For 473 days, the City's
actions have deprived the Movant of water service, resulting in an ongoing and severe
humanitarian crisis. The City incorrectly applies state tort notice requirements to federal civil
rights claims and mischaracterizes its series of actions as a single past event for timeliness
purposes. The Movant asserts a right to intervene to protect his possessory interest in his home,
viability of his federal claims, and timeliness of his state claims under the continuing tort doctrine.
The Movant further contends that the City's cynical position regarding the duration of the
deprivation does not preclude emergency relief. The Court should grant the motion in its entirety.

ARGUMENT

I. MOVANT IS ENTITLED TO INTERVENE UNDER ORCP 33

The City incorrectly claims Movant has no right to intervene. Intervention is proper under
ORCP 33 because Movant has a direct interest in the property, his interests are not adequately
represented, and his claims are viable.

A. The City Misconstrues the Standard for Intervention

Movant, as the lawful and continuous resident of the property since November 2023, holds a
direct and substantial possessory interest in his home. This is not a "mere consequential interest"
but a right to occupy the property at the center of this litigation. See Hannon v. Union High School
Dist. No. 2, 218 Or. 493, 502 (1959) (holding that an intervenor's interest must be direct, such
that they will "gain or lose by the direct legal operation of the judgment"). Movant stands to lose
his home if he cannot restore water service. The City's actions-shutting off the water without
due process, installing a concrete barrier, and posting an "Unsafe" notice-directly impair that
interest. This satisfies the standard for intervention under ORCP 33 A.

Furthermore, the existing parties cannot adequately represent Movant's unique interest in
resolving the daily health and safety crisis. Plaintiff Eriksson's claims focus on quiet title, while
the Bank Defendants are focused on foreclosure. Movant's priority is restoring the basic
conditions for life and health. This divergence of interests demonstrates that representation may
be inadequate, which is all the rule requires. See Taylor v. Portland Adventist Medical Center,
242 Or. App. 92, 101 (2011) (noting the minimal burden to show inadequacy of representation).
Movant's interest in immediate habitability is distinct and unprotected by the other parties.

B. Movant's Claims Are Not Futile

The City's central argument for denying intervention is the supposed futility of Movant's
claims. This argument fails because it ignores his viable federal claims and misapplies the law to
his state claims.

1. Federal Claims Are Expressly Exempt from OTCA Notice

The City's entire futility argument rests on the Oregon Tort Claims Act (OTCA). However,
Movant's Complaint in Intervention pleads multiple federal claims, including violations of
Procedural Due Process (42 U.S.C. § 1983) and the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA). It is
black-letter law that state tort claims notice requirements do not apply to § 1983 actions brought
in state court. See Felder v. Casey, 487 U.S. 131, 138 (1988). The City's own Response implicitly
concedes these claims are not subject to the OTCA notice requirement, as it attacks only the
timeliness of the tort claims. Because Movant's federal claims are not subject to dismissal on
timeliness grounds, the City's futility argument collapses. Intervention is warranted on the
strength of these claims alone.

2. The City's Conduct Constitutes a Continuing Tort

Even if the state tort claims were the only basis for intervention, they are timely. The City's
attempt to characterize its conduct as a single, discrete act is a gross misrepresentation of the
facts. The City engaged in a prolonged and escalating campaign of wrongful acts, with each act
renewing the tort and resetting the 180-day notice clock. This campaign includes:

* Wrongful water shutoff with same-day notice (June 25, 2024);
* Physical removal of the water meter (June 27, 2024);
* Installation of a permanent concrete barrier over the uncapped supply line (July 10, 2024);
* Refusal of good-faith payment offers (July 24 and August 27, 2024);
* Posting a retaliatory and intimidating "Unsafe" notice (August 4, 2024);
* Continuously billing for unusable sewer and stormwater services (through the current
  month of October, 2025).

The City's reliance on Boardmaster Corp. v. Jackson Cnty., 224 Or. App. 533 (2008), is misplaced.
That case involved a single order to terminate power. Here, the City did not commit one act, but
an ongoing series of affirmative, tortious acts designed to render the property uninhabitable.
Oregon courts recognize that a continuing tort exists "when recovery is sought for the cumulative
effect of wrongful behavior, not from discrete historical events." Barrington v. Sandberg, 164 Or.
App. 292, 296 (1999). The City's ongoing refusal to restore service, coupled with its new retaliatory
acts, constitutes a classic continuing tort, making the July 24, 2025 tort notice timely as to the
ongoing and most recent violations.

C. The Court Has Already Recognized the Hardships Justifying Any Delay

The City's procedural objections should be overruled for a more fundamental reason: this Court
has already set a precedent for excusing delays under the exact circumstances present here.
Earlier in this case, Judge Wetzel granted Plaintiff Eriksson's motion for leave to file a late
response, finding good cause based on the combined hardships of her pro se status, the complexity
of the litigation, and the severe stress caused by the ongoing property disputes. The reasons for
any delay in filing the present motion are identical, if not more severe. Movant is not merely
stressed by the litigation; he is living the emergency that necessitates it. The 473-day deprivation
of water has directly and profoundly impaired his health, his cognitive function, and his ability to
dedicate time and energy to complex legal filings. For the sake of consistency and in the interest
of justice, the Court should apply its own precedent, look past the City's procedural arguments,
and address the merits of the urgent humanitarian crisis at hand.

II. A TEMPORARY RESTRAINING ORDER IS URGENTLY NEEDED

A. The Duration of Harm Accentuates, Not Negates, the Emergency

The City's most callous argument is that because Movant has managed to survive for over
400 days without water, there can be no "immediate and irreparable harm" warranting a TRO. This
logic is perverse. The U.S. Supreme Court recognized that "the cessation of essential services for
any appreciable time works a uniquely final deprivation." Memphis Light, Gas & Water Div. v.
Craft, 436 U.S. 1, 20 (1978). The irreparable harm-including exposure to sewer gas
contamination, the severe exacerbation of diagnosed medical conditions, and the constant threat
to life and health-does not diminish with time; it compounds. The City's own Building Inspector
declared the home a "Dangerous Building" precisely because the lack of water creates a condition
"likely to cause sickness or disease". The City cannot use the duration of its own wrongful conduct
as a shield to perpetuate a dangerous situation.

B. Movant Has a Strong Likelihood of Success on the Merits

As established above, Movant's claims are strong. The City's same-day disconnection notice
is a clear violation of procedural due process. In Memphis Light, the Supreme Court held that due
process requires, at a minimum, notice and "an opportunity to present [one's] complaint to a
designated employee empowered to review disputed bills and rectify error." 436 U.S. at 21. A
notice posted the same day service is terminated is constitutionally deficient because it provides
no such opportunity. The City's dismissive response to Ms. Eriksson's ADA accommodation
request, which directly harmed Movant as a disabled co-resident, is strong evidence of an ADA
violation. These claims are not barred by the OTCA and are likely to succeed.

III. THE REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE IS PROPER

The city's argument that the movant cannot request judicial notice because he is not yet a
"party" is a classic example of a Catch-22. A proposed intervenor should be permitted to submit
evidence to the court justifying their request to become a party. This request is made in support
of the motion to intervene and is therefore procedurally proper. Furthermore, the city's blanket
challenge to the authenticity of its own official notices and public records is not made in good
faith and should be disregarded.

CONCLUSION

The city's response fails to rebut the compelling grounds for intervention and the urgent
need for a TRO. The court should reject the city's attempts to hide behind procedural arguments
and address the ongoing crisis. Respectfully, the Movant requests that the Court grant the Motion
in its entirety, allow intervention, issue an ex parte TRO to restore water service, and set an
expedited show-cause hearing.
