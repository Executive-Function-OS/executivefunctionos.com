## CHAPTER 1

### Origination, Broker Structuring, The Unlicensed Trustee, and the Engineered 80% LTV Appraisal (October 2006)

On October 26, 2006, Elbert G. Garboden and Sherry L. Garboden entered into a residential financing transaction for the acquisition and refinance of 12054 Chapin Court, Oregon City, OR 97045. The financing structure was arranged by wholesale mortgage broker Decision One Mortgage Company, LLC, employing an "80/20" simultaneous second-lien configuration designed specifically to provide 100% total financing while circumventing mandatory Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI).

The primary senior lien was memorialized by an Adjustable Rate Note in the stated principal amount of $281,600.00. To justify this principal amount within conventional underwriting guidelines without credit enhancement, the transaction required a total property valuation of exactly $352,000.00 (281,600 / $352,000 = 80% LTV).

This precise threshold was supplied by an appraisal prepared by Darren Harper of Harper Appraisal Service on October 24, 2006. The appraisal certified the property at exactly $352,000.00 in "as is" condition. Forensic review reveals this appraisal was an engineered accommodation valuation: comparable sales were selected and adjusted to match the predetermined financing threshold, ignoring incipient physical deferred maintenance. This allowed Decision One to originate high-margin non-conforming paper and immediately offload the risk into Wall Street securitization pipelines.

Parallel to the engineered appraisal, the closing mechanics executed by Decision One were infected by fatal statutory and corporate irregularities. On October 26, 2006, the Garbodens executed the primary security instrument, a Deed of Trust in favor of Mortgage Electronic Registration Systems, Inc. (MERS) solely as nominee for Decision One, which was subsequently recorded in Clackamas County Official Records on October 31, 2006 (Fee No. 2006-100973). In Section (D) of the instrument's definitions, Decision One designated the nominal trustee as:

> "SECURITY TITLE GUARANTEE CORP., Six South Calvert Street, Baltimore, MARYLAND 21202-1388"

Simultaneously, at 11:22 AM on October 31, 2006, closing title paperwork was finalized under a "Supplemental Preliminary Report for Title Insurance" (Order No. PO000545). The title report bore the letterhead of an entity styled "SECURITY TITLE GUARANTY CO.," listing an Oregon branch office at 10121 SE Sunnyside Road, Suite 265, Portland/Clackamas, OR 97015, prepared by Senior Examiner Deanna Wright (dwright@stgco.com) for proposed insured Decision One.

Forensic examination of state regulatory databases reveals that this title and trusteeship structure was a total legal fiction under Oregon law. Official records of the Oregon Department of Consumer and Business Services, Division of Financial Regulation (DFR), and the Oregon Secretary of State Business Registry confirm that neither "The Security Title Guarantee Corporation of Baltimore" nor "Security Title Guaranty Co." held a Certificate of Authority to transact title insurance in Oregon in 2006, nor was either entity registered as an active foreign corporation in the state.

Under ORS 86.713(1) (formerly ORS 86.790), an entity serving as trustee of an Oregon trust deed must strictly qualify under one of five statutory categories: an active Oregon State Bar member, an authorized financial institution or trust company, an escrow agent licensed under ORS 696.505 to 696.590, the United States, or a "title insurance company authorized to transact generally a title insurance business in this state, or any of its authorized title insurance agents in this state." Because the entity named on the recorded Deed of Trust satisfied none of these statutory qualifications, Decision One executed and recorded a security instrument naming an unauthorized out-of-state corporate stranger that had neither legal capacity under the Oregon Trust Deed Act nor knowledge of the transaction. From the date of its recordation, the Garboden Deed of Trust was burdened by a dormant statutory trustee defect that precluded lawful non-judicial exercise and cast an uncorrected cloud across the chain of title.

### The CWALT 2006-OC11 Securitization, The Three-Note Physical Mutation, and the Negotiable Instrument Forgery Audit (December 2006–Present)

Within weeks of origination, Countrywide Home Loans, Inc. packaged the Garboden Note into a massive private-label residential mortgage-backed security (RMBS). The securitization vehicle was created pursuant to a Pooling and Servicing Agreement (PSA) dated as of December 1, 2006, creating the Alternative Loan Trust 2006-OC11, Mortgage Pass-Through Certificates, Series 2006-OC11. CWALT, Inc. acted as Depositor, Countrywide Home Loans as Seller and Master Servicer, and The Bank of New York (now BNY Mellon) as Trustee.

The official PSA was filed with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission on Form 8-K on January 10, 2007 (Commission File No. 333-131630-79, Accession No. 0000905148-07-000100, Exhibit 99.1). Under Sections 2.01 and 2.02 of the PSA, the Depositor was required to deliver to the Trustee, on or prior to the December 28, 2006 Closing Date, the complete original Mortgage File, including the original Promissory Note endorsed in blank and an unbroken chain of interim assignments in recordable form.

While Oregon appellate courts have held that a borrower generally lacks standing to enforce private PSA compliance (*Deutsche Bank Trust Co. Americas v. Walmsley*, 277 Or. App. 690, 374 P.3d 937 (2016)), the legal defect in this case does not depend upon contract principles of the PSA. Rather, the complete failure of trust delivery is substantiated by the physical spoliation and alteration of the underlying Promissory Note itself under the Oregon Uniform Commercial Code—specifically ORS 73.0301 (Person Entitled to Enforce an Instrument) and the binding judicial foreclosure evidentiary standard established in *Bank of America v. Carlson*, 298 Or. App. 505, 447 P.3d 507 (2019), which mandates that a foreclosing plaintiff must prove it possessed the genuine, authenticated original note at the time the complaint was filed.

## CHAPTER 3

### The 2008 Payment Shock and the Unincorporated 2009 Countrywide Modification (2008–2009)

The 2006 Note was executed as an "InterestFirst" Six-Month LIBOR Adjustable Rate Note. Under Section 2 and Section 4 of the Note, the initial interest rate was fixed at 5.990% for only the first six months. The interest rate was subject to semiannual adjustments pegged to the Six-Month LIBOR plus a high margin of 4.990%, subject to a lifetime rate cap of 11.990% and a contractual floor equal to the margin of 5.990%.

In 2008, as the global financial crisis erupted, LIBOR spiked, causing the Garbodens' monthly payment to adjust steeply upward. Unable to sustain the payments, the borrowers defaulted. On January 16, 2009, Countrywide's "HOPE Team" issued a formal, written Loan Modification Agreement offer. The agreement offered to capitalize delinquent interest and escrow advances, establishing a newly restructured principal balance of $284,163.43, fixing the interest rate at 5.000% for five years, and stepping down monthly payments to $1,525.45.

However, the modification was never fully consummated or recorded. On March 3, 2009, a Countrywide internal "Master File Change Request" was generated (Batch 005, Seq 0001), which left the new loan term, interest rate, and maturity date blank. Countrywide failed to execute the final countersigned agreement. Crucially, when Bank of America acquired Countrywide, its servicing platform never updated the loan master file: the principal balance remained locked at the pre-modification figure of $264,151.04, creating an uncorrected accounting fault line that persisted for the next fifteen years.

## CHAPTER 9

### The Clackamas County Foreclosure (Case 17CV38591): Standing Failure Under Carlson & Extrinsic Fraud on the Court (2017–2019)

On December 6, 2017, attorney Jeffrey A. Myers filed a judicial foreclosure complaint in Clackamas County Circuit Court (Case No. 17CV38591). The action was fundamentally compromised by fatal standing defects under the Oregon Uniform Commercial Code and infected by actionable extrinsic fraud on the court.

First, under the binding judicial foreclosure standard established in *Bank of America v. Carlson*, 298 Or. App. 505, 447 P.3d 507 (2019), a plaintiff must prove that it was the lawful holder and possessed the authentic original note at the time the complaint was filed. Here, the named Plaintiff was *"The Bank of New York Mellon... as Trustee for the Certificateholders of the CWALT, Inc., Alternative Loan Trust 2006-OC10"*—a complete legal stranger to the transaction. The recorded 2012 assignment, the MIN registry, the 2014 FHFA settlement, and all servicer records confirmed the loan belonged to the sister trust, Alternative Loan Trust 2006-OC11. This was not a mere misnomer or "technical defect": BNYM-OC10 never possessed the note, held the assignment, or had any beneficial interest in the mortgage at filing, depriving the court of subject-matter standing under *Carlson*.

Second, to secure an uncontested judgment, counsel executed classic **extrinsic fraud on the court**—the exact category of structural deceit that vitiates the *Rooker-Feldman* doctrine under *Lewis v. Wells Fargo Bank NA* (D. Or. 2023) and renders a resulting judgment void for lack of personal jurisdiction (*Cohen v. Portland Lodge No. 142*, 140 F. 774 (D. Or. 1905)):
1. **Service Evasion:** Summons was deliberately directed to an abandoned, unassociated address rather than being served on Turning Leaf Homes' registered agent (Tracey Baron), whose actual address was known to counsel from prior representations.
2. **Corporate Identity Fabrication ("nka FITE, LLC"):** At the February 27, 2019 summary judgment hearing, Myers represented to Judge Breithaupt that TLH was "now known as F-I-T-E, LLC." This statement was demonstrably false: official Secretary of State filings and county land records (such as 18797 Blue Ridge Dr) establish that FITE, LLC was a completely separate corporate entity. Myers deployed this fiction to evade service on Baron and manufacture an uncontested default against his former client.
3. **Unauthorized Entry & Undisclosed Conflict (ORPC 1.9):** Myers entered the case on behalf of the adverse foreclosing bank without filing a statutorily required Notice of Substitution of Counsel (ORS 9.380) while operating under an undisclosed, severe conflict of interest against his former institutional client TLH, whom he had represented on an $8,000/month retainer.

Finally, to procure the judgment amount, Bayview Document Coordinator Keli Smith submitted a sworn declaration on August 31, 2018 falsely asserting that the loan carried a fixed interest rate of 7.375%, concealing the variable LIBOR terms and ARM floor adjustments. Based on these fabricated figures and post-dismissal procedural maneuvers, a default General Judgment of Foreclosure was entered on July 30, 2019—four days after the case had been formally dismissed.

## CHAPTER 9A

### The Collapse of Oregon Foreclosure Defense — How the Banks Closed the Door (2012–2022)

To understand why this case has proceeded without counsel — why every procedural irregularity, every fraudulent assignment, every servicing ledger discrepancy described in this manuscript was assembled and argued by a layperson working alone from a kitchen table — it is necessary to understand what happened to the Oregon foreclosure defense bar in the decade following the housing crisis. It did not slowly wind down. It was eliminated.

In the years following the 2011 national robo-signing revelations and the twin Oregon Supreme Court victories in *Brandrup v. ReconTrust* and *Niday v. GMAC Mortgage* — cases that established MERS-based non-judicial foreclosures were categorically defective under Oregon law — the legal profession briefly became crowded with foreclosure defense practitioners. An industry saying circulated at the time: at any legal conference, if you stopped two out of three attorneys and asked what they were practicing, the answer would be foreclosure defense. The legal community had recognized what appeared to be a structural opportunity. The Oregon Supreme Court had spoken. The defects were real. The cases were numerous. And the banks, for a brief moment, were exposed.

That window closed quickly. The reason was not that the legal theory proved wrong. The reason was payment.

Foreclosure defense, as a practice category, operates almost entirely on the wrong side of the fee structure. Homeowners facing foreclosure are, by definition, in financial distress. They do not have retainers. The equity they hold in their homes — the only available source of a fee recovery — has frequently been eroded or eliminated by the very events at issue. An attorney who wins a case completely, achieving a judgment that the foreclosure was void and the lender is owed nothing, may still find that the client's home, in a depressed post-crisis market, carries insufficient equity to compensate a legal team for the hundreds of hours of case preparation required to get there. When experienced attorneys who had spent years in the field were able to finally close that arithmetic, most of them left.

One such attorney was consulted personally during the initial year of this case. Paul Luby, who had represented the homeowner in *Niday v. GMAC Mortgage*, listened carefully to the facts of this matter in the period when the first post-foreclosure quiet title action was being prepared for filing in Clackamas County. His advice was not encouraging. He cautioned — explicitly and directly — against proceeding. His concern was not that the claims lacked merit; it was that the court, confronted with a pro se quiet title action filed after a completed judicial foreclosure, might treat the effort as harassment and impose sanctions. He had seen what the courts were doing with these cases, and he was not optimistic.

Phil Querin, perhaps the most prominent Oregon foreclosure defense commentator of the crisis era, had by then retreated into something that could only be described as exhaustion. When reached during the same consultation period, he was dismissive — not from malice but from the kind of professional battle-weariness that follows years of watching a legitimate legal argument absorbed and neutralized by an institution with unlimited resources. He had nothing useful to offer.

The Oregon State Bar's referral system was eventually exhausted. Every attorney on the list had either declined the case or was no longer practicing in the area. The bar had effectively run out of referrals. The observation this produces is not hyperbolic: there are no strictly foreclosure defense attorneys left in Oregon in the sense that the term implies a dedicated practice area. The attorneys who remain willing to engage with these cases do so peripherally, usually as one-off consultations within broader real estate, bankruptcy, or civil litigation practices.

The one institution still formally professing to help homeowners in legal distress is Elemental Law's Homeowners Legal Defense Project, directed by Hope Del Carlo. She spoke with the plaintiff in this matter on several occasions over multiple years. She was thoughtful, clearly committed, and genuinely sympathetic. Eventually, she delivered an assessment that was as blunt as it was financially precise: the merit of the case was not in question. What was in question was arithmetic. The number of attorney hours required to intake, review, and litigate a case of this complexity — spanning twenty years of transaction records, three servicers, a CWALT securitization trust, a contested foreclosure judgment, and a federal civil rights action — would exceed the entire market value of the property itself, even under a scenario in which the plaintiff prevailed completely and owed nothing to the bank. Her program could not take the case. No program built on traditional fee recovery could take the case.

That is the economic trap. The banks did not need to be right. They only needed to be expensive.

The broader mechanism by which the Oregon foreclosure defense bar was neutralized deserves documentation here, because it was not accidental. Following *Brandrup* and *Niday*, lenders and servicers largely abandoned non-judicial foreclosure in Oregon. They shifted instead to judicial foreclosure — a process governed by different statutory provisions, one that the Oregon Supreme Court rulings did not reach in the same way, and one that, by design, is more expensive and time-consuming to challenge. In the judicial foreclosure context, the servicer is represented by institutional lenders' counsel with full staffing, appellate experience, and cost indifference. The homeowner faces years of litigation before any dispositive ruling. The asymmetry is total.

The industry did not slay the *Brandrup* doctrine. It simply found the door behind it and walked through.

**The Brandrup Irony**

The attorney who won *Brandrup v. ReconTrust* — who argued before the Oregon Supreme Court that MERS-based nonjudicial foreclosure violated Oregon's Trust Deed Act and secured a homeowner victory that temporarily transformed foreclosure defense across the state — was Jeffrey A. Myers.

Jeffrey A. Myers is also the attorney of record who prosecuted Case No. 17CV38591, the Clackamas County judicial foreclosure proceeding that produced the disputed judgment against 12054 Chapin Court, Oregon City. He appeared on behalf of the lender. He signed the pleadings. He obtained the judgment.

The irony deepens substantially upon examination of Myers' record within the specific ecosystem of cases surrounding this property.

Court filings confirm that Myers previously represented RenX Group, LLC — an affiliate of Turning Leaf Homes, the entity that had acquired title to the Chapin Court property from the Garbodens — in a Washington County foreclosure defense proceeding (Case No. C151603CV), approximately contemporaneous with the filing of the 17CV38591 action. In that prior representation, Myers argued on behalf of RenX Group that the foreclosing plaintiff in that case "is not the real party in interest to initiate judicial foreclosure."

That is the foreclosure defense argument in its most fundamental form. It is the argument that the *Brandrup* doctrine was built upon. It is the argument that homeowners' attorneys spent years advancing in Oregon courts.

Myers then turned and advanced the diametrically opposite position — that his new institutional client was the real party in interest and was entitled to foreclose — in the proceeding against Turning Leaf's successor. The argument he had wielded as a shield became, in his hands, a sword.

## CHAPTER 13A

### Oregon Municipal Assessment Lien Law — Statutory Framework, Super-Priority Status, and Procedural Defects

The City of Oregon City's code enforcement campaign against 12054 Chapin Court did not occur in a legal vacuum. It operated within a specific and powerful statutory framework that grants Oregon municipalities extraordinary collection powers—powers that fundamentally alter the strategic landscape of the foreclosure defense and federal civil rights litigation.

**The Super-Priority Rule: ORS 223.230(3)**

The foundational statute is ORS 223.230(3), which provides that all unpaid final assessments, together with accrued interest and penalties, constitute a lien on the property "in favor of the local government, and the lien shall have priority over all other liens and encumbrances whatsoever." This language is unambiguous. Oregon law grants municipal assessment liens super-priority status—a status that leapfrogs even first-position deeds of trust.

This is not a theoretical proposition. The Oregon Supreme Court's decision in *State v. Vickery*, 299 Or 315 (1985), is directly on point. The Court determined that a City of Portland nuisance abatement assessment lien had priority over a prior recorded mortgage, expressly reversing the lower court's "first in time" ruling. The Court held: "We hold that the City's lien has priority and reverse the Court of Appeals." The *Vickery* decision confirms that the municipal liens arising from the City of Oregon City's code enforcement actions would, if validly perfected, take priority over the Bank of New York Mellon's first mortgage — a result with profound implications for the quiet title and foreclosure defense strategy.

**The Foreclosure Context: ORS 18.995 and the "Owner" Definition**

A critical distinction exists between liens arising from general municipal assessments and liens arising in the specific context of foreclosed residential real property. ORS 18.995 addresses this distinct scenario. The statute defines "foreclosed residential real property" as residential property that an owner obtains as a result of foreclosing a trust deed or obtaining a judgment foreclosing a lien on the residential property. Critically, the statute shifts the maintenance responsibility to the "owner" — defined as the entity that forecloses, not the original homeowner. The local government bears a notification duty to alert this "owner" of any violation.

This definitional framework is significant for the Chapin Court matter. If the 2019 foreclosure judgment is ultimately voided, the question of who constitutes the "owner" for purposes of ORS 18.995 obligations becomes a live issue.

**Notice Requirements: ORS 312.400 and the 21-Day Rule**

ORS 312.400 establishes mandatory notice requirements for municipal lien foreclosure. The statute provides that whenever the city treasurer posts or publishes notice of sale for delinquent assessments, the treasurer must give notice to any mortgagee or other lienholder who has filed a request for such notice, by registered or certified mail with return receipt. The notice must be mailed not less than 21 days prior to the date fixed for the sale.

If the Bank of New York Mellon (or its servicer) had filed a request for notice under ORS 312.390, and the City failed to provide the statutorily mandated 21-day notice, the resulting foreclosure sale could be challenged as procedurally defective.

**Strategic Implications: The Lien Priority Double-Edged Sword**

The super-priority status of municipal assessment liens creates a strategic paradox for the Chapin Court litigation:

1. **Procedural Defects Are the Strongest Angle:** The most viable challenges to the municipal liens are not to their existence or priority — which is statutorily mandated and court-confirmed — but to the validity and enforcement procedures. The notice defects under ORS 18.995 and ORS 312.400 are the most promising avenues for challenge.

2. **The Federal Complaint Remains the Priority:** The federal civil rights claims for retaliation, due process violations, and disability discrimination are separate from and can coexist with the state-law lien issues.

3. **The Municipal Lien Question Is a Longer-Term Battle:** Even if the federal action succeeds in preventing immediate enforcement, the lien priority question will likely require separate state-court litigation to resolve.

**The Doctrine of Futility and the Manufactured Decay**

The municipal lien framework intersects with the Doctrine of Futility documented in Chapter 12. The cloud on title created by the servicers' foreclosure litigation actively barred access to government weatherization and insulation grants under federal Department of Energy and state OHCS guidelines. This Catch-22 — where public funds cannot be expended on homes subject to active foreclosure litigation — directly caused the structural decay that municipal actors later weaponized through code enforcement.

This chain of causation is the factual predicate for the federal civil rights claims alleging municipal predation and unconstitutional taking. The super-priority lien framework is the collection mechanism through which the consequences of the servicers' misconduct were weaponized against the homeowner by a municipality that was on notice of the underlying causation.
