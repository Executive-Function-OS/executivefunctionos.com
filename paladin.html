01 — Structural Inevitability & Real Property Law

The Rules Were Never Repealed

The parallel between the 19th-century frontier range wars and 21st-century mortgage securitization is not poetic. It is structural.

For thirty years, modern financial architecture operated on a single core assumption: that physical real property could be converted into frictionless, high-frequency digital securities traded across global clearinghouses in nanoseconds without local recording fees, without physical deed books, and without human accountability.

Real property refused.

The physical soil, the immobility of the ground, the GPS coordinates, the negotiable instrument under UCC Article 3, and the public county recording acts still impose an inescapable friction that pure financial capital cannot erase. When the debt goes bad and the foreclosure machine scrambles back into a local county courthouse to seize the home, it must step out of the frictionless digital ether and enter the ancient, rigid domain of real property law.

“The absentee owner of natural resources is enabled to make them a source of free income, that is to say make them assets, by the power legally conferred on him to withhold them from use until his charge for their use is allowed him.”

• Thorstein Veblen, Absentee Ownership and Business Enterprise in Recent Times (1923)

“Modern property rules increasingly turn land into an asset class for monetization, capitalization, and exploitation—encompassing absenteeism, concentration, commodification, financialization, and even dispossession.”

• Prof. Jessica A. Shoemaker, Papering Over Place: When Land Becomes Asset Class, in Research Agenda for Property Law (Edward Elgar, 2024)

The ancient rules were never repealed. The industry simply tried to route around them.

They established MERS to bypass the county deed recorder. They executed Lost Note Affidavits to paper over missing negotiable instruments. They automated robosigning to fabricate chains of title that were never recorded. But the moment a self-represented homeowner stands before a judge and demands strict proof of standing, production of the original negotiable instrument under UCC Article 3, and an unbroken recorded chain of assignments under the state recording acts, the ancient common-law formalities reassert their full legal authority.

Securitization Without Compensation THE SYSTEMIC WIND-FALL

Securitization without compensation is not a theoretical abstraction—it is an uncompensated transfer of sovereign risk. When a mortgage loan is originated, the homeowner signs a promissory note and deed of trust. The originator and sponsor immediately bundle, slice, and sell those future cash flows into secondary market trusts, capturing immediate upfront liquidity, secondary trading premiums, and underwriting windfalls.

“Property law rules that once balanced the rights of mortgagors and mortgagees now completely fail to furnish aggrieved homeowners with meaningful relief when faced with wrongs that stem from the complexities of the securitization of mortgage loans and the acts of intermediaries... once a loan is securitized, its expectancy was realized by the sale of the securitized loan leaving some successor or servicer to benefit from windfall fees and profits largely in excess of or established outside the original contract.”

• Prof. Christopher K. Odinet, Modernizing Mortgage Law, 100 N.C. L. Rev. 89 (2021)

The borrower’s payment stream is fully capitalized and monetized on global markets, yet the borrower receives zero compensation, zero share of the secondary market liquidity, and zero equity participation in the structured product created from their promise. Instead, the homeowner bears 100% of the downside risk: predatory servicing fees, clouded title chains, and the catastrophic loss of their domestic hearth.

Furthermore, as documented by Adam J. Levitin and Tara Twomey in Mortgage Servicing (28 Yale J. on Reg. 1, 2011), servicer compensation models create a severe principal-agent conflict: servicers have no equity stake in the underlying property and do not share the investors’ or homeowners’ interest in maximizing loan preservation. Because servicers generate substantial revenue from late charges, inspection fees, and foreclosure fees, their operational incentives are systematically skewed toward dispossession rather than loan modification or restitution.

Intangible Human Potential vs. The Physical Hearth THE COLLATERAL CONTRAST

The profound difference between securitized debt structures becomes glaring when contrasting student loan asset-backed securities (SLABS) with residential mortgage-backed securities (RMBS):

When a student loan is securitized, the creditor can garnish wages and leverage statutory collection remedies—but they cannot repossess your education. They cannot erase your degree, repossess your intellect, or evict you from your mind. The debt is unsecured; the underlying “asset” is intangible human potential.

In mortgage securitization, however, the financial machinery treats physical real property as a sacrificial, liquidatable buffer. Yet how do RMBS certificateholders actually get paid?

In an RMBS trust, cash flows are distributed through a structured waterfall protected by credit enhancements—overcollateralization, subordination tranches, and servicer advances. Under standard Pooling and Servicing Agreements (PSAs), the servicer is contractually obligated to advance delinquent principal and interest payments to the trust for 120 to 180 days out of its own operating funds. Senior certificateholders continue receiving scheduled distributions regardless of whether a specific borrower is in default.

Displacing a family from their home is not required to pay senior certificateholders on day one. Foreclosure is the servicer’s mechanism to liquidate physical land to reimburse its own corporate advances and extract administrative penalties. Dispossession is treated as an operational settlement mechanism of an absentee corporate machine.

Integrity Over Friction: Technology Must Serve Truth PROCEDURAL ACCOUNTABILITY

PALADIN is not a reactionary plea for horse-and-buggy paperwork, nor do we oppose modern technological conventions such as Remote Online Notarization (RON), digital filing systems, or the Uniform Real Property Electronic Recording Act (URPERA). Technological modernization is vital. But efficiency cannot be purchased by abandoning evidentiary integrity.

A legislative review of remote notarization found that RON “creates new vulnerabilities for document fraud if electronic seals and authentication processes are not properly validated at the point of recording.” When electronic seals lack cryptographic validation, illegitimate documents acquire the veneer of authentic public recordation, making subsequent judicial challenge extraordinarily difficult.

“Thousands of borrowers lost their homes when employees of mortgage servicers notarized affidavits supporting scores of foreclosures without the signers present.”

• National Notary Association, Forensic Review of the Foreclosure Crisis & Consent Judgments

The lesson of the $25 Billion National Mortgage Settlement is clear: when financial institutions use technology as an excuse to eliminate human review and automate affidavits, the integrity of the record is compromised. PALADIN emphasizes verifiable standards, multi-factor credential analysis, and tamper-evident cryptographic verification. Technology must be used to prove the truth, not to paper over the absence of personal knowledge.

The Myth of the “Indefensible” Foreclosure & The Cost of Defense INSTITUTIONAL PERSPECTIVE & LIVED TRUTH

For decades, many participants in the legal process—encompassing private practitioners, high-volume legal aid programs, and overburdened court dockets—have operated under a defeatist assumption: that mortgage foreclosure cases are fundamentally “indefensible.” When distressed homeowners seek help, they are frequently steered toward capitulation: sell the home, refinance the debt, or file bankruptcy. Rarely does an advocate carefully read the foreclosure complaint, audit the recorded chain of title, or demand admissible proof of holder standing under the UCC.

“Most lawyers believe foreclosures are not defensible. Rather than working to save a home, most lawyers acting in good faith advise clients to sell their homes... Even when given in good faith, this is poor advice. Many foreclosures can be defended... Inherent in lawyers’ refusal to try to defend foreclosure cases are deep-seated prejudices against homeowners who fall into foreclosure... The difference between losing and saving her home was a competent, determined foreclosure defense.”

• Kelli Dudley, An Excerpt of Iniquity: How Court Systems, Attorneys, and Legal Aid Organizations Cheated Homeowners in Foreclosure, 12 DePaul J. for Soc. Just. (2019)

As Chicago legal aid attorney Kelli Dudley documented in her landmark analysis, homeowners are systematically written off under paternalistic “deadbeat” stereotypes that treat due process violations as inconsequential. In reality, as Dudley proved in her 15-year battle in Chancery Court on behalf of homeowner Wilma L., rigorous title defense, consolidation of eviction actions, and aggressive quiet title counterclaims can defeat fraudulent transfer schemes and clouded titles to secure unencumbered ownership.

Correspondence with Kelli Dudley, Former Foreclosure Defense Attorney June 2024 • DePaul / Chicago Legal Clinic

“I am no longer practicing law. I was threatened with jail for my efforts on behalf of several tenants referred to as ‘squatters.’ Since I cannot give specific legal advice and do not know any Oregon lawyers... a better resource for you might be American Property Owners Network... I have no doubt you have experienced the due process violations and other wrongs you mention—this is, unfortunately, very common.”

— Written to PALADIN Founder Annika Eriksson in response to her outreach regarding An Excerpt of Iniquity.

This exchange lays bare the harrowing institutional reality: when an attorney actually stands and fights on behalf of occupants dismissed as “squatters” or “deadbeats,” the systemic backlash is severe enough to drive dedicated advocates from the courtroom under threat of incarceration.

When institutional resources fall short, when busy trial court dockets push for rapid throughput, and when self-represented filers must navigate complex evidence rules alone, reliable procedural tooling becomes an essential public resource. PALADIN is built to equip that litigant with source-linked legal-information tools, institutional memory, and procedural rigor.

The Standing Trap: Frivolous Myths vs. Statutory Bedrock STRATEGIC DISCIPLINE

The greatest tragedy in modern pro se litigation is not that homeowners lack defenses; it is that desperate litigants are seduced by pseudo-legal internet folklore. For years, sovereign-citizen theories, “vapor money” fallacies, and generalized “show me the note” slogans have flooded state and federal courts. Federal appellate courts have consistently and properly dismissed these frivolous claims for lack of concrete injury, causation, and redressability (see, e.g., Garner v. Wells Fargo Home Mortgage, Inc., 505 F. App'x 837 (11th Cir. 2013); Carter v. PrimeLending, No. 3:25-cv-00653 (W.D.N.C. 2026)).

A homeowner cannot prevail merely by asserting that a promissory note and deed of trust were separated, or that securitization itself invalidates an underlying debt. Courts demand legal precision.

Paraphrasing the synthesis of post-crisis case law: Where a homeowner perceives a defect in securitization but cannot demonstrate a break in the chain of lawful title or concrete prejudice arising from that defect, generalized challenges fail as a matter of law. The statutory conditions precedent — proof of enforcement standing, authenticated records, and adequate indemnity — are the only reliable path to relief.

• Elizabeth Renuart, Uneasy Intersections: The Right to Foreclose and the U.C.C., 48 Wake Forest L. Rev. 1205 (2013) (paraphrased synthesis; not a direct quotation)

PALADIN draws an uncompromising line against pseudo-law. We arm the defender with strict, black-letter statutory requirements that institutional syndicates frequently violate:

  • UCC § 3-301 / ORS 73.0301 (Person Entitled to Enforce): Compelling admissible proof that the foreclosing entity held the indorsed note at the exact moment the action commenced.
  • UCC § 3-309 / ORS 73.0309 (Lost Instrument Evidentiary Gate): Strictly barring hearsay “lost note affidavits” unless the claimant proves possession when lost, details the facts of the loss, and posts adequate indemnification protection — requirements that automated sub-servicer declarations routinely fail to satisfy.
  • OEC 803(6) (Prior-Servicer Data Boarding Foundation): Striking robo-signed declarations where corporate affiants testify to records created by prior defunct servicers without personal knowledge of their record-keeping systems (In re Taylor, 407 B.R. 618; In re Weisband, 427 B.R. 13).
  • County Recording Acts: Enforcing state recording statutes over unrecorded internal MERS ledger entries. MERS member rules acknowledge that beneficial ownership transfers are tracked privately rather than recorded in county deed books — a practice that state recording acts did not authorize and courts continue to scrutinize.
